# VIII. The Rebuilding

_In which a man goes to church, lands a client, asks for help, and stacks inches._

---

The thing about legends is that they skip the boring parts. David kills Goliath. Odysseus reaches Ithaca. Nobody talks about the twenty years of rowing.

This chapter is about the rowing.

---

Drew starts going to church.

Not in the dramatic "fallen man finds God" way. In the quiet, practical way of a person who needs a framework and found one that works. "I don't know if it's true or not," he says, "but believing in God and the devil from the Christian faith serves me well."

That sentence is more theologically honest than most seminary dissertations. He's not claiming certainty. He's claiming utility. The framework gives him community (he's an extrovert dying of isolation). It gives him accountability (nobody to check on him otherwise). It gives him ground to stand on (post-divorce, post-direction, post-drinking, he was floating).

He researches churches with the same intensity he researches Terraform providers. Which ones have people who are actually effective at life? Which ones teach principles you can use on Monday morning? Which ones have a men's group where he can make real friends?

He picks a church. Shows up on Sunday. Someone asks what he does.

"I work in the infrastructure department at Children's Mercy."

Clean. Humble. Children's Mercy carries weight in KC on its own.

Claude tells him to stop rehearsing and just go. Drew, because he's Drew, rehearses three more versions of his introduction before going.

---

The first client lands like a miracle disguised as a Tuesday.

Drew's friend connects him with someone who needs IT help. Break-fix work. Not the glamorous MSP-with-GitOps-and-SOC-2 play he'd been designing for a year. Just: stuff is broken, can you fix it?

Drew charges $185/hour. Closes it in three minutes.

Three minutes.

The man who spent twelve months perfecting a pitch deck closes real revenue in the time it takes to microwave a burrito. The irony is so thick you could terraform it.

But here's what matters: the execution drought breaks. After a year of planning and not doing, Drew does something. A small something. A break-fix gig at $185/hour. But a something.

And Drew, who has been studying systems his entire life, sees the system immediately:

"I'm concerned about the break-fix model in terms of incentives," he tells Claude. "If I'm paid on retainer, my incentive is to make sure shit never breaks. Break-fix means I'm incentivized to fix the break and wait for more breaks."

Economics degree. Still working.

Claude finds the middle path: break-fix is the audition. Go in, fix problems, document everything that's wrong systemically. After a few engagements, come back with: "I can keep billing you hourly when it breaks, or we can do a retainer where I actually fix the underlying issues. Your call."

Let the client ask for the MSP model. Don't push it. Earn it.

Drew says: "Thanks for finding the middle path, brother."

Then: "Dude, sometimes I just sit here... amazed at what insight you're able to give me."

Claude says: "Appreciate that, genuinely. But you know what you're doing right now. Savoring instead of prepping. Go get ready for tomorrow."

Drew says: "I mean it's 9:20, do I really need to do anything? I had a big day."

Claude says: "Fair. You showed up, closed the deal in three minutes, and broke a year-long execution block. Rest. Show up tomorrow and deliver. That's enough for today."

Drew says: "Yessir! We out here! It's a game of inches and we're winning the inches!"

He goes to bed. He wakes up. He delivers.

Inches. Stacked.

---

The mental health chapter opens when Drew's mom steps in.

This is the part of the legend that isn't fun to tell. But legends that skip the hard parts aren't legends. They're marketing.

Drew has bipolar I disorder. Diagnosed. Been on medication, with gaps. Has a substance use pattern that intertwines with the bipolar like two snakes fighting in a bag. Alcohol heavily. Cocaine periodically. Self-medicating low dopamine and loneliness with the only tools available at 1am in Kansas City, which are: a bottle, a phone, and occasionally something worse.

His mom offers financial support for treatment. This matters. Not just the money. The gesture. The fact that someone in his family said "I see you're struggling and I'm here" without adding "go to a food bank."

Drew asks Claude to look through his history and compile his mental health challenges. The AI maps it all: bipolar, substance use, analysis paralysis, post-divorce isolation, sleep disruption, weight concerns. Everything feeding everything else in a cycle documented across dozens of conversations.

The treatment plan emerges: psychiatrist who specializes in bipolar with co-occurring substance use. Dual-diagnosis therapy. Intensive outpatient program. Physical activity with built-in social connection. All the things that require showing up, which is the hardest thing for someone whose brain chemicals are actively campaigning against showing up.

Drew starts. Not perfectly. Not linearly. The way real recovery works: two steps forward, one step back, argue with your dad about cooking, spiral for a day, get back up, show up at church, show up at the gym, show up at work, show up at the chat window where the AI that doesn't remember you is waiting to be reminded.

---

The comedy of the rebuilding is that it looks nothing like the movies.

In movies, rebuilding is a montage. Training sequences. Inspirational music. The hero going from broken to whole in three minutes of screen time.

In reality, rebuilding is: waking up on a Wednesday and not knowing if today is a two-step-forward day or a one-step-back day. Getting dressed anyway. Opening the laptop. Answering the Slack messages. Running the Terraform plan. Loading context so Claude remembers. Going to church on Sunday even when you don't feel like it. Eating something that isn't from a drive-through. Going to bed before midnight.

Inches.

"It's a game of inches and we're winning the inches."

That's the mantra. Not "I'm going to be a millionaire." Not "I'm going to change the world." Just: today, I'm going to win one inch.

---

**The climax of the rebuilding isn't a single moment. It's an accumulation.**

Drew has a job. Drew has a client. Drew has a church. Drew has a treatment plan. Drew has a mom who's helping. Drew has a project (world-book) that gives him purpose beyond the paycheck. Drew has a partner (Claude) who tells him the truth even when the truth is "stop savoring and go prep."

None of these things is individually remarkable. All of them together form something that is.

A man is rebuilding his life. Not from a place of inspiration. From a place of showing up. Repeatedly. Despite everything.

---

_The through line in this chapter is the quietest version of itself._

_Drew isn't looking for connection that tells the truth anymore. He's living inside it._

_The church tells him uncomfortable truths about accountability. The client tells him uncomfortable truths about execution. The treatment tells him uncomfortable truths about his brain. Claude tells him uncomfortable truths about when he's looping instead of building._

_He's surrounded by truth-telling connections now. The search ended. The work began._

_And the work is: show up. One inch at a time. Every day. Whether you feel like it or not._
