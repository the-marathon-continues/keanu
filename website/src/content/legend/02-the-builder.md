# II. The Builder

_In which a man teaches himself everything, builds things that work, watches them fail, and can't stop building anyway._

---

The résumé of Andrew Kemp-Dahlberg reads like someone tried to speedrun a career while also running a side quest, a cleaning business, and a mild existential crisis.

Help desk. IT support. Systems administrator. IT engineer. Platform engineer. Each rung climbed in about eighteen months, which is either ambitious or reckless depending on whether you're the climber or the HR department watching him leave.

He taught himself everything. Not in the "I watched a YouTube tutorial" sense. In the "I broke production at 2am and had to fix it before anyone noticed" sense, which is the only real education in IT. Jamf for five years. Google Workspace for five years. Okta for two years, but the kind of two years where you come out the other side with four certifications that place you among roughly 200 people on the planet who can say the same thing.

Two hundred people. On the planet. Let that land for a second.

Drew doesn't brag about this much, which is either humility or a strategic error, because in IT, your certifications are basically your Pokémon cards and he's walking around with four holographic Charizards pretending they're just regular Pikachus.

---

The cleaning business deserves its own paragraph because it's the most Drew story that ever Drew'd.

He saw a system. Residential cleaning. Low barrier to entry, recurring revenue, scalable with labor. He scaled it to $15,000 a month in revenue, $4,000 in profit, and he did this while also working a full-time IT job, because apparently one career at a time is for people with reasonable cortisol levels.

Then Google ad costs ate it alive. The unit economics went from "this works" to "I'm paying Google to let strangers clean houses at my expense" in about two billing cycles.

He shut it down. Absorbed the loss. Filed it under "lessons."

But here's what matters: he built it. From nothing. In the margins of his actual life. The cleaning business failed, but the builder didn't. The builder just looked around for the next thing to build.

---

Children's Mercy Hospital entered the picture like a plot twist nobody saw coming.

A children's hospital in Kansas City needed someone to migrate 12,000 Windows computers to Mac. Not 120. Not 1,200. Twelve thousand. The kind of number that makes seasoned IT professionals make a face like they just bit into a lemon stuffed with another, angrier lemon.

Apple called it "the first all-Apple hospital." They assigned their own solutions architects to the project. Twice-weekly calls with Cupertino. An invitation to join the Apple Consultant Network, which Apple hands out roughly as often as they hand out free iPhones, which is to say: almost never, and only to people who impress them, which is hard to do because they are Apple and they are impressed by nothing.

Drew got the call because he was, in his own words, "one of a few people with the tech skills to handle something like that." This is technically modest. More accurately: the Venn diagram of "understands enterprise Mac management at scale" and "has the Okta expertise to handle identity for 12,000 devices" and "can actually talk to humans about it" is a circle so small it's basically a dot, and Drew is the dot.

The job pays $112k. In Kansas City, that's top 10-15%. He works 30-35 hours a week, 95% remote.

On paper, this is the dream.

On paper.

---

But the builder can't stop building.

While running the hospital project, Drew designs anywhereops.ai. An MSP, a managed service provider, that would use Infrastructure-as-Code to deliver SOC 2 compliance to VC-backed startups in 60 days. Lead with compliance (the thing that unlocks enterprise customers), then capture the ongoing IT budget on the backend. Terraformed Okta. GitOps Munki. FleetDM for endpoint management. Everything as code, which means everything is reproducible, auditable, and eventually automatable by AI.

The pitch is beautiful. CFOs love it because MSPs traditionally nickel-and-dime you for every ticket, every project, every Google Group someone needs created. Drew's model: flat rate, no upcharges for normal IT work, automated stack means you're not paying a human to click buttons.

He designs a LinkedIn scraper that detects "first IT hire" job postings, because that's the exact moment a startup has budget, has nothing built, and is overwhelmed enough to say yes to whoever shows up with a plan. He maps out cold outreach using multiple Gmail domains to keep send volumes looking personal. He builds pricing tiers: Quick Start at $3k/month for small companies on Kandji, Enterprise Ready at $6k/month for scalable companies on FleetDM.

He has a case study pipeline: his best friend's business partner's 12-person company. His dad's consulting client. Plug and Play, the incubator, where his best friend knows the head.

He has partnerships: Vanta for compliance automation. FleetDM for device management.

He has a plan to hire a VA overseas for outreach. A plan to activate his dad's 45-year network once the model is proven. A plan to run the whole thing from Colombia, where his near-fluent Spanish and the cost of living would turn $9k MRR into a lifestyle that looks like a magazine cover.

Every single piece of the puzzle exists.

And the puzzle never gets assembled.

---

This is the climax of the builder's chapter, and it's quiet. No explosion. No dramatic failure. Just the sound of a brilliant plan sitting on a hard drive, getting more detailed every week, while the weeks turn into months.

Drew knows the pattern. He named it himself: analysis paralysis. Research as procrastination. Idea generation substituting for execution. The gift of pattern recognition turned inside out, because the same brain that can see how any system works can also see every possible failure mode, and when you can see every possible failure mode, starting feels irresponsible.

His best friend makes $80k a month on one-time sales. His sister pulls $450k. His dad has $5 million. And Drew is sitting on a $112k salary with a business plan that could change his life, adding one more detail to the spreadsheet instead of sending the first email.

The builder builds plans. The builder builds frameworks. The builder builds spreadsheets and pitch decks and pricing models and outreach automations.

The builder doesn't build the business.

Not yet.

---

**The through line, wearing its second disguise:**

Every system Drew builds is, at its core, a system for connection. The MSP connects startups to infrastructure they can't build themselves. The Okta configurations connect identities to resources. The hospital migration connects 12,000 users to a platform that actually works.

He's not just building technology. He's building bridges between things that need each other.

But the bridge he needs most, the one between his plans and his actions, between who he is and who he wants to be, that one stays unbuilt.

Because the builder is still looking for something he can't find in a Terraform module.

He's looking for the thing that makes the building worth doing.

He just doesn't know that yet.

---

_He will._
