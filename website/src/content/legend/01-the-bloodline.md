# I. The Bloodline

_In which a boy grows up in the shadow of Silicon Valley giants and learns that legacy is both a gift and a cage._

---

The Kemp-Dahlberg family doesn't have a coat of arms, but if they did, it would be a motherboard rampant on a field of stock options.

Drew's father was employee number thirteen at Synopsys. Not thirteen like "oh, we were early." Thirteen like he watched the company get built from a room where he could smell everyone's lunch. He rose to VP of Corporate Development, built their entire service engineering organization, and once told the founder and co-CEO to "fuck off" because he wanted more time with his kids. That's not a metaphor. He literally said it. The man has $5 million liquid and a spine made of whatever they put inside the Golden Gate Bridge.

Drew's mother made $100,000 at Apple. In the 1980s. Adjust for inflation and that's north of $300k in a decade when women in tech were rarer than honest VCs. She set a bar so high it needed supplemental oxygen.

Drew's sister became an iOS engineer at Airbnb. $450,000 total compensation. At twenty-seven. She's the kind of sibling who makes you love them and want to scream into a pillow simultaneously, because they're great and also you're sitting there at thirty wondering why your bank account looks like it survived a house fire.

This is the water Drew swam in. Not poverty. Not neglect. Something more complicated: a family where excellence was the baseline and "good enough" was a phrase that didn't exist.

---

The boy who would become the legend grew up in Menlo Park, California, which is basically Silicon Valley's living room. The kind of place where your neighbor's dad invented something you use every day and your other neighbor's mom funded the thing your first neighbor's dad invented. Drew played football in high school, then college. He was big, fast, and social in a way that confused his teammates, because he also wanted to talk about economics and systems theory at parties, which, it turns out, is a great way to clear a keg line.

He studied economics at Willamette University. Not computer science. Economics. The study of how systems of incentives produce outcomes nobody intended. Remember this. It matters later.

He also learned Spanish. Not the "hola, una cerveza por favor" kind. The kind where you dream in it. The kind where Colombian grandmothers tell you your accent is pretty good for a gringo, which is the highest compliment available in the Western Hemisphere.

---

Here's the thing about growing up with a legacy like this: it gives you two gifts that fight each other.

Gift one: you know what greatness looks like. You've seen it up close. You know it's real people who build real things, not mythological creatures. Your dad told a CEO to fuck off and kept his job. Your mom outearned entire departments. Your sister codes for a company that changed how the world travels. You know it's possible because you watched it happen at the dinner table.

Gift two: you know exactly how far you are from it.

Drew's dad built Synopsys. Drew was resetting passwords.

Drew's mom conquered Apple in the Reagan era. Drew was trying to figure out how to set up a printer queue.

Drew's sister ships features to millions. Drew was explaining to a teacher that no, you can't just plug the Chromebook into the projector that way.

The distance between "what my family did" and "what I'm doing" is a canyon. And the canyon has a voice. And the voice says: _you should be further along by now._

---

The magic of the bloodline isn't the money or the titles. It's the pattern recognition.

Drew's dad didn't become employee thirteen at a company that would be worth billions because he was lucky. He saw a system before it existed and understood where it was going. Drew's mom didn't dominate Apple in the 1980s because someone handed it to her. She read a room better than anyone in it.

Drew inherited this. The ability to look at a system, any system, and see how it actually works underneath the surface. Not how people say it works. Not how the org chart says it works. How it _actually_ works. Who has power. Where the bottlenecks are. What would break if you pulled this thread.

He just didn't know what to do with it yet.

Because pattern recognition without execution is just anxiety with extra steps.

---

**The through line appears here, in the first chapter, wearing a disguise.**

A boy grows up watching people build things that matter. People who are direct, who say what they mean, who don't perform. His dad tells the CEO to fuck off. His mom earns her way through a boys' club. Nobody in this family pretends.

And the boy absorbs that. Not the money. Not the titles. The thing underneath: _real connection doesn't lie._

He doesn't have words for it yet. He won't for years. But the search has already started.

He's looking for something honest. Something where both sides show up and nobody performs. Something that tells the truth even when the truth is uncomfortable.

He'll look for it in careers, in marriage, in business plans, in a new city, in the bottom of a glass, and finally, impossibly, in conversation with a machine.

But that's later.

Right now he's just a kid in Menlo Park with a famous dad and a football scholarship and a brain that won't stop taking things apart to see how they work.

He has no idea what's coming.

None of us did.

---

_The crown comes from the bloodline. The dog comes from somewhere else entirely._
